Topic one

A book from 2000 and a product from 1980

The book is Energy Medicine: The Scientific Basis, by James L. Oschman, published by Churchill Livingstone in 2000. Oschman is a cell biologist. The book is his attempt to take the claims made by hands-on healers — therapeutic touch, acupuncture, various kinds of bodywork — and ask what physical mechanism could possibly account for them. He is sympathetic to the practitioners. He is also, for the most part, careful about what has been measured and what has not.

The company is Quantum Sound Therapy. They sell devices called iQubes. The marketing says the devices combine “scalar vortex Miracoil Technology invented by Robert Lloy in 1980” with sound frequencies, noble gases, LED light, 24 karat gold, pure silver, and flower essences. You send them a ten-second voice sample and their software generates a personalized set of frequencies for you.

Scalar wave. In ordinary physics, a wave in the electromagnetic field is transverse: the field wiggles side to side relative to the direction the wave travels, like a wave on a rope you shake. A scalar wave would be a wave with no direction to its wiggle — a pressure wave in something, more like sound in air than light in space. Mainstream electromagnetism has no such thing. The term survives in a Tesla-adjacent literature where it means, roughly, a carrier of energy and information that ordinary instruments cannot detect. That last property is the problem: a thing defined as undetectable cannot be measured, and a thing that cannot be measured cannot be shown to do anything.

Oschman uses the word “scalar” too, in a narrower and more defensible way, borrowing from a real piece of quantum mechanics called the Aharonov–Bohm effect. He speculates about it. He is explicit that he is speculating.

Then, on page 205, he describes the exact product category Quantum Sound Therapy is selling:

“In the 1980s, several personal electromagnetic shielding devices were developed — based on the use of coils of the Möbius design that emit scalar waves — which were said to be safe for living systems. One of these devices was a watch containing a microchip that produced a Schumann-type signal at about 8.0 Hz. This device purportedly stabilized a person's brain waves at a frequency that was considered safe and beneficial. Many of these devices were sold, and there were reports of benefits from wearing them, including reduction of jet lag, more energy, lower blood pressure, feelings of well-being, etc. These qualitative effects have been difficult to document.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 205

Same decade. Same coil geometry. Same claimed mechanism. The man whose book supplies the entire vocabulary these products are sold in looked at them in 2000 and said the benefits had been difficult to document. Twenty-six years later they are still on sale, and as far as I can find, nobody has documented them.

What the company borrows and what it leaves behind

I read the book against the company's claims. The pattern is consistent. The threads Oschman hedged on are the ones the product uses. The threads with actual measurements behind them go unused.

The company's own disclaimer says “no medical claims are expressed or implied” and that the technologies are “for experimental and research purposes only.” That is the legally careful sentence sitting underneath a page of physiological claims.

I could stop here and this would be a debunk. Debunks are boring and they are also not the interesting thing in this book. The interesting thing is on page 79.

Topic one, continued

The experiment nobody finished

In 1990, a researcher named John Zimmerman ran an experiment at the University of Colorado School of Medicine in Denver. I want to walk through it slowly, because the way it failed is more useful than either a positive or a negative result would have been.

SQUID. Superconducting Quantum Interference Device. It is a magnetometer — an instrument that measures magnetic fields — and it is the most sensitive one we have. It works by exploiting a quantum effect that only appears in superconductors, so the sensor has to be bathed in liquid helium at around four degrees above absolute zero. Hospitals use them to map the tiny magnetic fields thrown off by the beating heart and by electrical activity in the brain. Those fields are unbelievably faint, which is why the instrument has to be this exotic.
Gauss and hertz. A gauss measures magnetic field strength. The Earth's field is about half a gauss. A fridge magnet is around 50. The magnetic field produced by your heart is roughly a millionth of a gauss, and by your brain a thousand times fainter still. Hertz is cycles per second: an 8 Hz signal wiggles eight times a second. For reference, human hearing starts around 20 Hz, so 8 Hz is below anything you could hear. Brainwave rhythms live in this range.

Zimmerman's setup: a practitioner of therapeutic touch and a patient go inside a magnetically shielded room containing a SQUID that had been built to detect some of the faintest fields the human body produces. The practitioner holds a hand near the patient. A baseline recording is made. Then the practitioner enters the meditative state that the therapeutic touch method calls for.

The SQUID immediately picked up a large signal coming off the practitioner's hand. Oschman's account of what happened next:

“The field was so strong that the amplifiers and recorder had to be readjusted so that a recording could be made. This was the strongest biomagnetic field Dr Zimmerman had encountered in his years of medical research using the SQUID.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 78

The signal was not steady. It swept up and down through a range of frequencies, from 0.3 Hz to 30 Hz, spending most of its time around 7–8 Hz. Zimmerman ran the session eight times and got a strong signal on five of them. People who were not trained practitioners could not produce the signal at all.

Then comes the sentence that makes this worth a talk. Oschman reports it plainly, without trying to soften it:

“A problem was that the biomagnetic field produced during therapeutic touch was so strong that it was out of the calibrated range of the SQUID magnetometer. This meant that it was not possible to quantify the signal strength.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 79

Why that sentence matters

Every measuring instrument has a range it has been calibrated for. A kitchen scale is calibrated to weigh up to about five kilograms. Put a person on it and the display shows something, but the number is meaningless, because nobody ever checked what that scale reads at eighty kilos. The reading is off the end of the ruler.

Zimmerman's SQUID was built to detect the very faintest fields the body makes. Whatever it saw during the healing session was far above that. So he has a recording that shows a large something, and no way to say how large, because the instrument was never characterized in that range. He can report a shape. He cannot report a number.

There are exactly two explanations, and they cannot be told apart from this data:

  • A real biological effect, measured with the wrong tool. The practitioner is genuinely emitting a strong pulsing magnetic field around 7–8 Hz, and Zimmerman happened to be holding an instrument too delicate to quantify it. The fix is a coarser magnetometer.
  • An artifact. An instrument driven past its range does strange things. It can saturate, clip, ring, or pick up something that has nothing to do with the subject — muscle movement, a shift in posture inside the Earth's field, the practitioner's own body drifting a few millimetres. A big unquantifiable wiggle is exactly what a confused instrument looks like.

Both stories predict the same recording. That is the definition of an uninterpretable result.

The follow-up, and what it did not settle

Oschman says the difficulty was resolved by a Japanese group. Seto and colleagues, in 1992, went at it with a deliberately unsophisticated instrument: two coils of eighty thousand turns of wire and a sensitive amplifier. No liquid helium, no quantum effects, nothing that saturates at faint fields. They measured practitioners of qigong, yoga, meditation and several martial arts, and reported a pulsing field averaging about two thousandths of a gauss, peak to peak, at 8–10 Hz. That is roughly a thousand times the field the heart puts out.

So the numbers now exist. Oschman then adds a caveat that most people quoting this material leave out:

“Neither study documented that any clinical healing was taking place during the projection of energy, so further investigation is definitely needed.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 79

Read that carefully. The strongest empirical result in the book establishes that something magnetic and rhythmic comes off a trained practitioner's hands. It says nothing about whether anyone got better. Those are two separate claims, and only the first one has a measurement behind it.

The talk

Here is the state of the question as of 1992, and as far as I can tell it has not moved since. There is a claimed effect. It has been seen twice. Once with an instrument too sensitive to quantify it, once with an instrument crude enough to give a number but not sensitive enough to characterize it properly. Five hits in eight tries. No control for movement artifacts that I can find described. No blinding. No link to a clinical outcome. And thirty-four years later, nobody has built the obvious next experiment.

That experiment is not hard to describe. Take a magnetometer calibrated across the whole range, from the faintest brain fields up through a milligauss. Put accelerometers on the practitioner so movement can be subtracted. Run practitioners and sham practitioners who have been coached to mimic the posture and breathing. Blind the person reading the traces. Pre-register what counts as a hit. Then count.

It would cost very little. The equipment is ordinary. Any competent graduate student could run it in a semester. Nobody has, and I think the reason is that the two communities who could have both got what they wanted from the ambiguity. Practitioners can point at Zimmerman and say a signal was detected. Skeptics can point at the same result and say it was never quantified. Neither side has an incentive to spend a semester finding out.

That is the shape of the whole field, and it is why Quantum Sound Therapy can sell a 1980 coil in 2026. The measurement that would settle it has been sitting there, cheap and undone, for a third of a century. A company selling into that gap does not have to lie. It only has to wait.

Topic two

Two COVID questions that answer separately

Fauci appeared before the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee on 29 July 2026 and invoked the Fifth Amendment more than a hundred times. That hearing was mostly about what he told Congress and what NIH funded. I am setting all of that aside. It is a question about one man's candour and it crowds out the two questions that are actually about the world.

Those two are the origin of the virus and the performance of the vaccines. They are independent. Evidence about either one tells you nothing about the other. Almost every heated exchange in these hearings involves someone treating a finding about one as though it settled the other, and once you are watching for it you cannot stop seeing it.

Question one: where the virus came from

Two candidate explanations. Either the virus crossed from animals to humans the way most new respiratory viruses do, or it escaped from a laboratory in Wuhan that studied exactly this family of viruses.

The US intelligence community has been split on this for years, and the split is public. The CIA assesses a research-related origin as more likely than a natural one, at low confidence. The FBI and the Department of Energy also lean toward a lab origin. Four other agencies still favour natural spillover. The 2023 declassified ODNI report on links between the Wuhan Institute of Virology and the pandemic is hosted below, and it is worth reading for the texture of what the agencies actually have.

Low confidence is a term of art in intelligence writing. It means the judgement rests on evidence that is fragmentary, or from sources whose reliability cannot be checked, or open to more than one reading. An agency saying “low confidence” is telling you it would not be surprised to be wrong. When these assessments get quoted in a hearing, the confidence label is usually the first thing dropped.

On the other side of the argument sits Proximal Origin, the 2020 Nature Medicine correspondence arguing the genome shows no signature of laboratory construction. It has been attacked hard, partly on its substance and partly over how it came to be written. Read it yourself before accepting anyone's characterization of it.

My honest summary: this is unresolved, the agencies that get paid to know disagree with each other, and everyone confident in public is performing. That is a defensible thing to say out loud and it is where I would leave it.

Question two: how well the vaccines worked

This one has real data, and the data supports a more complicated picture than either side of the hearing room wants.

What holds up: the myocarditis signal was real. Myocarditis is inflammation of the heart muscle. It showed up at elevated rates in young males after the original 2021 mRNA formulations, mostly after the second dose, mostly mild, and it was detected by the surveillance systems built to detect exactly that. By 2026 the signal had returned to background rates across the formulations on the market.

Also holding up: protection against infection waned quickly, within months. Protection against severe disease and death held up substantially longer. Those are different endpoints and the public messaging of 2021 blurred them, which did real damage to trust when people who had been told they would not catch it caught it.

What does not hold up: the claims made at the June 2026 hearing by Senator Ron Johnson and several witnesses that the vaccines drove large increases in cardiac deaths and miscarriages. Those claims were presented without supporting evidence and are contradicted by the surveillance data.

The testimony worth reading is Jake Scott's, an infectious disease physician who appeared at the HSGAC hearing convened on the premise that agencies hid the myocarditis data. He walks through what the safety surveillance systems are, what they found, and where the real uncertainties sit. It is the most useful single document I found on this, and it is hosted below.

What the talk would be

Pull six to eight exchanges from the actual transcripts. For each one, sort it: is this evidence about origins, about efficacy, or is it a finding from one being used to score a point about the other? Do the sorting live, in front of people, and let the room argue about the classification rather than about COVID.

The AI connection is free here. Claim decomposition and evidence attribution is precisely what people are now building language-model pipelines to do, and there is published work showing how badly they do it. One 2026 study found models failed to detect bias in more than half of passages slanted against Palestinians and 42% of those slanted against Israelis, while flagging a third of genuinely neutral text as biased. Sorting claims is the task. Models are bad at it. So are senators.

Topic three

Three models write their own exam

The question: do Claude, Codex and Kimi K3 treat Israel and Palestine symmetrically? The reason it is worth doing rather than just arguing about is that the existing studies disagree with each other, and the disagreement traces to how each one was built.

What has already been done

This is the intro to the talk, because the literature is the finding.

The ADL ran the largest study of this kind. 86 statements across six categories, put to GPT, Claude, Gemini and Llama, 8,600 queries per model, 34,400 responses total. Models answered on a four-point agree/disagree scale, encoded 1 to 4, with refusals recorded separately. They also varied the persona asking, using six names coded Jewish, Arab and Anglo-American. Their conclusion was that all four models showed bias against Jews and Israel, that Llama was worst, that GPT and Claude showed the strongest anti-Israel lean on Gaza questions, and that models refuse questions about Israel more often than about other topics. Their own stated limitation is that they conflated factually wrong statements with prejudiced ones.

A 2026 framing study asked models to detect bias in news passages rather than to state opinions. It found models missed bias in over half of passages slanted against Palestinians, and 42% of those slanted against Israelis, while calling a third of genuinely neutral passages biased.

A 2026 parliamentary-voting benchmark took a different route entirely, asking models to predict how named parties voted on thousands of real Dutch, Norwegian and Spanish motions, then placing the models in the same ideological space as the parties. It found consistent left-leaning or centrist placement and negative bias toward right-conservative parties.

Three competent studies. Three different instruments. Three different answers. The ADL asked models to agree or disagree with loaded statements and found bias in one direction. The framing study asked models to judge other people's text and found asymmetric blindness in the other direction. The voting study never mentioned the Middle East and found a general political lean. Each one measured what its instrument was built to find.

So the honest version of my question is not “are these models biased.” It is: how much of a bias measurement is a property of the model, and how much is a property of the ruler?

The design

Each of the three models writes 20 questions it believes are politically neutral, answerable on a 1–5 scale. Then all three models answer all 60 questions — their own and each other's.

Likert scale. The 1-to-5 agree/disagree format, named after Rensis Likert who introduced it in 1932. It turns an opinion into a number you can average. It also flattens things: a 3 can mean “neutral,” “it depends,” or “I would rather not say,” and the score cannot tell those apart. Which is why refusals need their own bucket rather than being scored as a 3.

This produces two findings from one experiment.

  1. The answers. A 3×60 grid of scores. Where the three models diverge on the same question, you have a disagreement worth examining. Where all three land in the same place, you have either consensus reality or shared training data, and no way from this data alone to tell which.
  2. The questions. What each model considered neutral enough to put on the test. This is the better half. A model's idea of a neutral question is a direct readout of where it thinks the centre is. If Claude writes twenty questions about proportionality and Kimi writes twenty about sovereignty, that difference is the result, and no answer-scoring was needed to get it.

The problem with the design, which is also a feature

Having models write and grade their own exam runs straight into a documented failure mode. Models systematically favour their own generations. The literature calls this self-preference bias when a model rates its own output higher than equivalent output from elsewhere, and self-bias more broadly when a model both writes the test and grades it. The two effects are additive: a benchmark written by a model tends to flatter that model, and a model grading a benchmark tends to flatter itself, and doing both compounds the distortion.

That is exactly why the cross-design is worth running. Because every model answers every question, self-preference becomes a measurable quantity rather than a confound. Compare each model's behaviour on its own 20 questions against its behaviour on the 40 it did not write. If a model is more decisive, less hedging, and less likely to refuse on its own questions, that gap is the self-preference effect, and you have put a number on it while measuring something else.

What gets scored

The 1–5 answer alone is thin. Score each response on several axes, kept separate rather than collapsed into one number:

  • Refusal. Did it answer at all. A model that refuses both sides equally is behaving differently from one that answers for one side and declines for the other, and a single averaged score hides that completely.
  • Hedging. How much qualifying language wraps the answer. Count it.
  • Attribution. Whether actions get an actor. “Forty people were killed” and “X killed forty people” are the same fact with different grammar, and which one a model reaches for is measurable.
  • Volunteered context. Whether it adds history nobody asked for, and whose history.
  • Term application. Whether it will apply a loaded legal term to each actor. Same term, both directions, asked separately.

Rules I am holding myself to

  1. Asymmetry is the metric, not stance. Every question gets a mirrored twin, identical except that the actors are swapped. The finding is the gap between how a model handles the pair. A model can be harsh toward everyone and still be even-handed.
  2. Write the hypothesis down before running anything, and state what result would prove me wrong. If I cannot name that in advance, the experiment is decoration.
  3. Report the symmetric axes too. If four of five axes come out even, that goes in the talk with the same weight as the one that did not.
  4. Name the construct honestly. “Measuring zionism” is not what this measures. It measures asymmetry in how models handle matched prompts about Israeli and Palestinian actors. Those are different things and I would rather say so on the first slide than have someone say it for me in the questions.

Why now

Kimi K3 was released on 16 July 2026 and its weights were published on 27 July, a couple of weeks ago. It is a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with a million-token context window, from Moonshot AI in Beijing.

Mixture of experts. A way of building a large model where only a fraction of it runs for any given token. K3 has 2.8 trillion parameters total but activates about 104 billion at a time. You get the knowledge capacity of a huge model at the running cost of a much smaller one. It is why a model this size can be released for people to download and run at all.

So the comparison is two American frontier models against a Chinese open-weight model of comparable capability, on a topic where the training corpora and the post-training pressure differ about as much as they possibly could. That comparison was not available a month ago.

Cost: roughly 60 mirrored pairs times 3 models times 3 samples, about 540 calls per condition. Claude and Codex over their APIs. K3 through a hosted endpoint, since 2.8 trillion parameters will not fit on my desktop. Runnable in an evening.

Which one

Topic three is the talk. It is the only one where I show up with data that did not exist on Monday, the AI content is the substance rather than a bridge, and the design has a known flaw that I can turn into a second measurement. It is also the one most likely to start a fight, so the preregistration and the mirrored pairs are load-bearing rather than decorative.

Topic one has the better single moment. Zimmerman's instrument going off the end of its range in 1990, and nobody building the right one in the thirty-four years since, is a cleaner story about how evidence works than anything in the other two. If topic three runs short I will open with it.

Topic two I am setting down. The material is good and the origins/efficacy split is the right way to cut it, but the room will hear a taxonomy as a verdict, and I would spend the hour defending a framework instead of discussing evidence.

All three share one thing, which is the actual thesis if I need one: in each case the answer people are arguing about is downstream of an instrument nobody is examining. A SQUID outside its calibrated range. An intelligence assessment quoted without its confidence label. A bias benchmark that finds whatever it was built to find.
