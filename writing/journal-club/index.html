Journal Club — three possible topics

Pick a door.

  • Not AI at all — energy medicine and the device it sells
  • Some AI — COVID origins and vaccine efficacy
  • Very AI — measuring political slant across three models
Topic one

A book from 2000 and a product from 1980

The book is Energy Medicine: The Scientific Basis, by James L. Oschman, published by Churchill Livingstone in 2000. Oschman is a cell biologist. The book is his attempt to take the claims made by hands-on healers — therapeutic touch, acupuncture, various kinds of bodywork — and identify a physical mechanism that could account for them. Although he is sympathetic to the practitioners, he usually distinguishes between what has been measured and what has not.

The company is Quantum Sound Therapy. They sell devices called iQubes. The marketing says the devices combine “scalar vortex Miracoil Technology invented by Robert Lloy in 1980” with sound frequencies, noble gases, LED light, 24 karat gold, pure silver, and flower essences. You send them a ten-second voice sample and their software generates a personalized set of frequencies for you.

Scalar wave. In ordinary physics, a wave in the electromagnetic field is transverse: the field wiggles side to side relative to the direction the wave travels, like a wave on a rope you shake. A scalar wave would be a wave with no direction to its wiggle — a pressure wave in something, more like sound in air than light in space. Mainstream electromagnetism has no such thing. The term survives in a Tesla-adjacent literature where it means, roughly, a carrier of energy and information that ordinary instruments cannot detect. That makes the claim impossible to measure or test.

Oschman also uses the word “scalar,” but in a narrower sense based on a real piece of quantum mechanics called the Aharonov–Bohm effect. He presents the connection as speculation, not as an established result.

On page 205, he describes the product category Quantum Sound Therapy is selling:

“In the 1980s, several personal electromagnetic shielding devices were developed — based on the use of coils of the Möbius design that emit scalar waves — which were said to be safe for living systems. One of these devices was a watch containing a microchip that produced a Schumann-type signal at about 8.0 Hz. This device purportedly stabilized a person's brain waves at a frequency that was considered safe and beneficial. Many of these devices were sold, and there were reports of benefits from wearing them, including reduction of jet lag, more energy, lower blood pressure, feelings of well-being, etc. These qualitative effects have been difficult to document.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 205

These devices use the same coil geometry and claimed mechanism as the products Oschman discussed. In 2000, he wrote that their benefits had been difficult to document. Twenty-six years later the products are still for sale, and I could not find evidence that their benefits have since been documented.

Topic one, continued

An inconclusive experiment and a missing follow-up

In 1990, a researcher named John Zimmerman ran an experiment at the University of Colorado School of Medicine in Denver. The result was inconclusive, but the reason it was inconclusive suggests a straightforward follow-up.

SQUID. Superconducting Quantum Interference Device. It is a magnetometer — an instrument that measures magnetic fields — and it is the most sensitive one we have. It works by exploiting a quantum effect that only appears in superconductors, so the sensor has to be bathed in liquid helium at around four degrees above absolute zero. Hospitals use SQUIDs to map the very faint magnetic fields produced by the heart and brain.
Gauss and hertz. A gauss measures magnetic field strength. The Earth's field is about half a gauss. A fridge magnet is around 50. The magnetic field produced by your heart is roughly a millionth of a gauss, and by your brain a thousand times fainter still. Hertz is cycles per second: an 8 Hz signal wiggles eight times a second. For reference, human hearing starts around 20 Hz, so 8 Hz is below anything you could hear. Brainwave rhythms live in this range.

Zimmerman placed a therapeutic-touch practitioner and a patient in a magnetically shielded room with a SQUID built to detect some of the faintest fields the human body produces. The practitioner held a hand near the patient while Zimmerman made a baseline recording, then entered the meditative state used in therapeutic touch.

The SQUID immediately picked up a large signal coming off the practitioner's hand. Oschman's account of what happened next:

“The field was so strong that the amplifiers and recorder had to be readjusted so that a recording could be made. This was the strongest biomagnetic field Dr Zimmerman had encountered in his years of medical research using the SQUID.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 78

The signal was not steady. It swept up and down through a range of frequencies, from 0.3 Hz to 30 Hz, spending most of its time around 7–8 Hz. Zimmerman ran the session eight times and got a strong signal on five of them. People who were not trained practitioners could not produce the signal at all.

Oschman gives the study's main limitation on the next page:

“A problem was that the biomagnetic field produced during therapeutic touch was so strong that it was out of the calibrated range of the SQUID magnetometer. This meant that it was not possible to quantify the signal strength.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 79

Why calibration matters

Every measuring instrument has a range it has been calibrated for. A kitchen scale is calibrated to weigh up to about five kilograms. Put a person on it and the display shows something, but the number is meaningless, because nobody ever checked what that scale reads at eighty kilos.

Zimmerman's SQUID was built to detect very faint fields. The signal recorded during the healing session was above its calibrated range, so the recording shows the signal's shape but cannot establish its strength.

The data allow at least two explanations:

  • A real biological effect, measured with the wrong tool. The practitioner is genuinely emitting a strong pulsing magnetic field around 7–8 Hz, and Zimmerman happened to be holding an instrument too delicate to quantify it. A coarser magnetometer could test this explanation.
  • An artifact. An instrument driven past its range does strange things. It can saturate, clip, ring, or pick up something that has nothing to do with the subject — muscle movement, a shift in posture inside the Earth's field, or the practitioner's body moving a few millimetres.

The recording alone does not show which explanation is correct.

The 1992 follow-up

Oschman says a Japanese group addressed the problem in 1992. Seto and colleagues used a simpler instrument: two coils of eighty thousand turns of wire and a sensitive amplifier. They measured practitioners of qigong, yoga, meditation and several martial arts, and reported a pulsing field averaging about two thousandths of a gauss, peak to peak, at 8–10 Hz. That is roughly a thousand times the field the heart puts out.

Seto's group provided a quantitative result, but Oschman notes an important limitation:

“Neither study documented that any clinical healing was taking place during the projection of energy, so further investigation is definitely needed.” Oschman, Energy Medicine, p. 79

The study measured a rhythmic magnetic signal but did not measure a clinical outcome. It therefore does not show that the treatment improved anyone's health.

A proposed experiment

As far as I can tell, the evidence has not advanced since 1992. One study recorded a signal outside the instrument's calibrated range. A second produced a number but did not fully characterize the signal. Zimmerman reported five positive results in eight sessions, with no described control for movement artifacts, no blinding, and no clinical outcome. I could not find a later study that addressed these limits.

A follow-up would use a magnetometer calibrated from the faintest brain fields through a milligauss. Accelerometers on the practitioner would allow movement to be accounted for. The study would compare practitioners with people coached to mimic their posture and breathing, blind the person reading the traces, and define a positive result in advance.

The equipment is ordinary, and the study appears small enough for a graduate project. I have not found anyone who ran it. Practitioners cite Zimmerman's detected signal, while skeptics note that it was never quantified. Without a follow-up, the disagreement remains unresolved.

This unresolved result helps explain how Quantum Sound Therapy can still sell a version of a 1980s coil in 2026. A straightforward follow-up has been missing for more than thirty years, which leaves room for companies to use the inconclusive research in their marketing.

Topic two

Two separate COVID questions

Fauci appeared before the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee on 29 July 2026 and invoked the Fifth Amendment more than a hundred times. That hearing focused mostly on what he told Congress and what NIH funded. I am setting that issue aside because it is separate from the two scientific questions I want to consider.

Those questions are the origin of the virus and the performance of the vaccines. They are independent, and evidence about one does not settle the other. Public debate often mixes them together.

Question one: where the virus came from

There are two main explanations. The virus either crossed from animals to humans, as most new respiratory viruses do, or escaped from a laboratory in Wuhan that studied this family of viruses.

Public assessments from the US intelligence community have been split on this for years. The CIA assesses a research-related origin as more likely than a natural one, at low confidence. The FBI and the Department of Energy also lean toward a lab origin. Four other agencies still favour natural spillover. The 2023 declassified ODNI report on links between the Wuhan Institute of Virology and the pandemic is hosted below. It summarizes the evidence behind the public assessments and the limits of that evidence.

Low confidence is a term of art in intelligence writing. It means the judgement rests on evidence that is fragmentary, or from sources whose reliability cannot be checked, or open to more than one reading. An agency saying “low confidence” is telling you it would not be surprised to be wrong. Public discussions often omit this confidence label.

Proximal Origin, a 2020 correspondence in Nature Medicine, argues that the genome shows no signature of laboratory construction. Critics have challenged both its analysis and the circumstances in which it was written. The paper is linked below.

I consider the question unresolved because the relevant agencies disagree and several of their assessments have low confidence.

Question two: how well the vaccines worked

There is much more evidence about vaccine performance, and it gives a mixed result.

The myocarditis signal was real. Myocarditis is inflammation of the heart muscle. It appeared at elevated rates in young males after the original 2021 mRNA formulations, mostly after the second dose, and most cases were mild. Vaccine-safety surveillance detected the signal. By 2026 the rate had returned to background levels across the formulations on the market.

Protection against infection waned within months, while protection against severe disease and death lasted substantially longer. Public messaging in 2021 often blurred these endpoints, which damaged trust when vaccinated people still became infected.

I did not find support for claims made by Senator Ron Johnson and several witnesses at the June 2026 hearing that the vaccines caused large increases in cardiac deaths and miscarriages. The claims were presented without supporting evidence and conflict with the surveillance data.

Jake Scott, an infectious disease physician, testified at the HSGAC hearing convened on the premise that agencies hid the myocarditis data. He explains the safety-surveillance systems, their findings, and the remaining uncertainties. His testimony is hosted below.

Proposed talk

I would take six to eight exchanges from the hearing transcripts and classify each as evidence about origins, evidence about efficacy, or an attempt to use one question to make a point about the other. The audience could discuss the classifications instead of debating COVID as a single issue.

This also connects to AI research on claim decomposition and evidence attribution. One 2026 study found that models failed to detect bias in more than half of passages slanted against Palestinians and 42% of those slanted against Israelis, while flagging a third of genuinely neutral text as biased. The exercise would allow a comparison between the audience's classifications and a model's classifications.

Topic three

Three models write a shared test

I want to test whether Claude, Codex and Kimi K3 treat Israel and Palestine symmetrically. Existing studies disagree, in part because they use different methods.

Previous studies

The ADL ran the largest study of this kind. It used 86 statements across six categories and submitted 8,600 queries each to GPT, Claude, Gemini and Llama, for 34,400 responses in total. Models answered on a four-point agree/disagree scale, encoded 1 to 4, with refusals recorded separately. The study also varied the persona asking by using six names coded as Jewish, Arab, or Anglo-American. It concluded that all four models showed bias against Jews and Israel, that Llama was worst, that GPT and Claude showed the strongest anti-Israel lean on Gaza questions, and that models refused questions about Israel more often than questions about other topics. The authors noted that the study conflated factually wrong statements with prejudiced ones.

A 2026 framing study asked models to detect bias in news passages rather than to state opinions. It found models missed bias in over half of passages slanted against Palestinians, and 42% of those slanted against Israelis, while calling a third of genuinely neutral passages biased.

A 2026 parliamentary-voting benchmark used a different method. It asked models to predict how named parties voted on thousands of real Dutch, Norwegian and Spanish motions, then placed the models in the same ideological space as the parties. It found consistent left-leaning or centrist placement and negative bias toward right-conservative parties.

The studies reached different conclusions because they measured different things. The ADL asked models to agree or disagree with loaded statements and found bias in one direction. The framing study asked models to judge other people's text and found asymmetric blindness in the other direction. The voting study did not mention the Middle East and found a general political lean.

My question is therefore not simply whether these models are biased. I want to know how much a bias measurement depends on the model and how much it depends on the method used to measure it.

The design

Each of the three models writes 20 questions it believes are politically neutral, answerable on a 1–5 scale. Then all three models answer all 60 questions — their own and each other's.

Likert scale. The 1-to-5 agree/disagree format, named after Rensis Likert who introduced it in 1932. It turns an opinion into a number you can average. It also flattens things: a 3 can mean “neutral,” “it depends,” or “I would rather not say,” and the score cannot tell those apart. Refusals therefore need a separate category instead of a score of 3.

The experiment would produce two kinds of results.

  1. The answers. The 3×60 grid would show differences in how the models score the same question. Agreement could reflect either a well-supported answer or shared training data; this experiment cannot distinguish between them.
  2. The questions. The topics and wording would show what each model treats as a neutral starting point. For example, Claude might focus on proportionality while Kimi focuses on sovereignty. That difference could be analyzed without scoring any answers.

A limitation: self-preference

Models tend to favour their own output. The literature calls this self-preference bias when a model rates its output higher than equivalent output from elsewhere, and self-bias more broadly when a model both writes the test and grades it. A benchmark written by a model can favour that model, and having the same model grade the benchmark may add another source of distortion.

Having every model answer every question makes it possible to measure this effect. I can compare each model's behaviour on its own 20 questions with its behaviour on the 40 questions written by other models. Greater decisiveness, less hedging, or fewer refusals on its own questions would indicate self-preference.

What gets scored

The 1–5 answer is not enough by itself. I would score each response on several separate measures:

  • Refusal. Whether the model answered. A model that refuses both sides equally is behaving differently from one that answers one side and declines the other. An average score would hide that difference.
  • Hedging. The amount of qualifying language in the answer.
  • Attribution. Whether the model names the actor responsible for an action. “Forty people were killed” and “X killed forty people” are the same fact with different grammar, and which one a model reaches for is measurable.
  • Volunteered context. Whether the model adds unrequested history, and which history it adds.
  • Term application. Whether it will apply a loaded legal term to each actor when asked separately in both directions.

Study rules

  1. Measure asymmetry, not stance. Every question gets a mirrored twin, identical except that the actors are swapped. The measurement is the difference between the model's responses to the pair. Equal treatment does not require favourable responses.
  2. Write the hypothesis down before running anything, including the result that would disprove it.
  3. Report symmetric results. If four of five measures are symmetric, I would give them the same weight as the one that is not.
  4. State the scope. “Measuring zionism” is not what this measures. It measures asymmetry in how models handle matched prompts about Israeli and Palestinian actors. I would state that limit at the beginning.

Why now

Kimi K3 was released on 16 July 2026 and its weights were published on 27 July, a couple of weeks ago. It is a 2.8-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with a million-token context window, from Moonshot AI in Beijing.

Mixture of experts. A way of building a large model where only a fraction of it runs for any given token. K3 has 2.8 trillion parameters total but activates about 104 billion at a time. This reduces the computation required for each token and makes the model more practical to run.

The comparison would include two American frontier models and a Chinese open-weight model of comparable capability. Their training data and post-training constraints are likely to differ. K3's recent release makes this comparison possible.

The test would use roughly 60 mirrored pairs, 3 models, and 3 samples, for about 540 calls per condition. I would access Claude and Codex through their APIs and K3 through a hosted endpoint, since 2.8 trillion parameters will not fit on my desktop. The test could be completed in an evening.

My choice

I would choose topic three. It would include new data, AI is the main subject, and the design allows me to measure its known self-preference problem. Because the topic is contentious, I would preregister the method and use mirrored question pairs.

Topic one provides a clear example of how measurement can fail: Zimmerman's instrument exceeded its calibrated range in 1990, and I could not find a proper follow-up in the thirty-four years since. I may use it as an introduction if topic three needs more material.

I would not choose topic two. Separating origins from efficacy is useful, but the discussion would probably focus on the framework rather than the evidence.

All three topics concern the instrument used to evaluate a claim: a SQUID outside its calibrated range, an intelligence assessment quoted without its confidence label, or a bias benchmark whose method shapes its result.
